# Background Context  

Claude Query, 8/19/2026:  
  
“How well has the SLTTm 2004 vocabulary been accepted by the Earth Sciences community?”  
  
Response:  
  
SLTT — the Science Language Technical Teams convened under the North American Data Model Steering Committee (NADM), which produced the 2004 report your vocabulary is citing — has an interesting adoption story: it's simultaneously very influential and rarely used directly in its original form.

  

**Where it succeeded:** The SLTT sedimentary/metamorphic rock language (plus adopted igneous-rock standards) became the definitional backbone for a lot of what came after it. The SLTT report became the principal component of the National Geologic Map Database's lithology term list, and that restricted set of terms was subsequently incorporated into the GeoSciML standard lithology terms and definitions — which is the international standard your CGI SimpleLithology vocabulary descends from (you can still see "SLTTs 2004" cited directly in the sourced definitions for terms like sandstone, conglomerate, and clastic sedimentary rock). It also propagated into industry data standards — WITSML's lithology enumeration tables cite SLTTs 2004 directly in their rock-type definitions. 

Sources: 

  - <https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2009/1298/pdf/usgs_of2009-1298_soller4.pdf>
  - <https://energistics.org/sites/default/files/2023-03/WITSML_Lithology_Object_Usage_Guide_1.1Revisions%20Version%202.pdf>

  

**Where it struggled:** The full, original SLTT product was never actually published as a formal standalone standard — these vocabularies were never formally published, though they served as input for the international GeoSciML effort. And when someone tried to actually deploy the complete SLTT term set for real users, it didn't hold up: a synthesis of the SLTT vocabularies into a single lithologic classification produced 2027 terms, and project experience building a user interface and testing it with geologic users demonstrated that this list was too large and the relations among terms too complex to be successfully used without considerable training. That's what pushed the NGMDB team to build a deliberately smaller, simplified derivative vocabulary for their public-facing Data Portal rather than exposing the full SLTT hierarchy to end users. 

Sources:

  - <https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2010/1335/pdf/usgs_of2010-1335_Richard.pdf>
  - <https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2009/1298/pdf/usgs_of2009-1298_richard1.pdf>
      
      - Quote (p103): “Science language technical teams formed under the auspices of the North American Data Model Steering Committee (http://nadm-geo.org/sltt/) developed vocabularies for sedimentary and metamorphic rocks, and adopted existing standards (Streckeisen, 1976) for use with igneous rocks (NADM-SLTT, 2004; http://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2004/1451/nadm). Our synthesis of these vocabularies into a single lithologic classification produced a vocabulary with 2027 terms. Over the past several years, project experience develop ing a user interface to utilize this vocabulary, and testing it with geologic users, demonstrated that this list is too large and the relations among terms too complex to be successfully utilized without considerable training. Based on this experience, we determined that a smaller vocabulary would be necessary for integrating geologic maps displayed through the NGMDB Data Portal.”

  

**Bottom line for your purposes:** SLTT is well-accepted as an authoritative source of definitions — it's the thing everyone cites and builds on (GeoSciML, NGMDB, WITSML all trace back to it) — but it's essentially never been adopted wholesale as an end-user-facing vocabulary, because its full hierarchical complexity proved unusable without training. That's directly relevant to what you're doing: you're not the first group to find that the SLTT-derived hierarchy (like the chert/biogenic-silica polyhierarchy question from earlier) needs pragmatic simplification or restructuring for real users — that's essentially the same lesson NGMDB learned, and it's why GeoSciML's simplified SimpleLithology vocabulary exists as a derivative rather than just re-publishing SLTT as-is.
